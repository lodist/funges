# Soil pH from SoilGrids in Europe, and three US fixes

Date: 2026-10-05

The European score read soil pH from a map that cannot tell chalk from peat, so
the pH term did nothing in North Europe: every plant there had been fitted to
an optimum of pH 4.5–4.8, the region's background value. This record covers
the switch to SoilGrids, the refit of every European species' pH term, three
US values that pointed the wrong way and three European altitude optima. It is
the source the changed manifests cite in `scoringReferences`.

**The values below are agent-derived.** They take effect only when the PR that
adds this record is merged.

## The European pH map

`ph_level` came from the JRC "Map of Soil pH in Europe" (Reuter et al. 2010;
5 km, pH in CaCl2), extracted outside the repository. The extraction is
spatially correct: the raster has no projection file, but 95.5% of its pixels
fall on the land of the bundled EPSG:3035 country map. The values are the
problem.

| place (expected pH in water)  | JRC (CaCl2) | SoilGrids (water) |
| ----------------------------- | ----------: | ----------------: |
| Salisbury Plain, chalk (~7.5) |         3.9 |               6.8 |
| South Downs, chalk (~7.5)     |         4.9 |               6.4 |
| Burren, limestone (~7)        |         3.9 |               5.4 |
| Öland alvar (~7.5)            |         4.6 |               6.3 |
| Gotland, limestone (~7.5)     |         5.1 |               6.7 |
| Dutch marine clay (~7)        |         4.8 |               7.2 |
| Scottish Highlands peat (~4)  |         4.0 |               4.4 |
| Swedish boreal forest (~4.5)  |         5.2 |               4.8 |
| Finnish forest (~4.5)         |         4.5 |               5.0 |

JRC rates the chalk and limestone places 4.76 on average and the acid ones
4.65. SoilGrids rates them 6.33 and 4.92. At the 66,936 North Europe points,
the two maps have a Spearman correlation of 0.08.

The acid lovers must come out below 0.5 and the lime and nitrogen lovers above

## What changed

**The European static file.** `backend/tools/build_eu_soil_ph.py` rebuilds
`ph_level` from SoilGrids 2.0 (`phh2o_15-30cm_mean_1000.tif`: pH in water,
15–30 cm, 1 km), the same layer the US static file already uses. Every fetched
coordinate lends its pH to all base points mapped to it, about 13 in North
Europe and 8 in South Europe, so a coordinate takes the median over those
points rather than the one pixel under it. Nothing else in the file changes.
It is published as `EU/EU_static_info_soilgrids.csv`, and `.env` points
`EU_STATIC_INFO` at it. The old file stays on R2 for a rollback.

| region | coordinates | pH missing before | after |
| ------ | ----------: | ----------------: | ----: |
| NE     |       5,171 |              3.2% |  0.3% |
| SE     |      13,420 |             30.7% |  0.3% |

A point without pH is scored without the pH term, so 31% of South Europe had
none.

**The data sources page** credits SoilGrids for Europe and the US and no
longer lists the JRC map.

## How each value was chosen

**The optimum** is the median pH, on the new map, at a species' GBIF sightings in
the region (2014–2026, human observations, coordinate uncertainty ≤ 1 km). Each
sighting takes the pH the score would give it: that of the nearest base point's
fetched coordinate, across North and South Europe together. Wild asparagus has
no North Europe sightings and borrows South Europe's optimum. The summer
truffle (10 sightings) keeps pH 7.8, already a pH-in-water value for limestone
soils.

**The spread** was chosen with production scoring, following the protocol of
the 27 September calibration: `calculate_mushroom_score` on the regional
parquet weather, season months set to all twelve and no season curves, scored
at 2026 sightings (12 April – 5 October). The measure is how often a sighting
outscores a random point on the same day. The random points are limited to
where the species can grow: range prior × host prior ≥ 0.5, as production
gates it. Each species was scored with today's map and values, and with the
new optimum at four spreads: today's (1.5, or 2.5 for wood-decay fungi), 1.0,
2.5 and 5.0 (nearly flat).

The rule: today's spread with the new optimum, unless another spread ranks
sightings at least 0.01 better. With fewer than 50 sightings in 2026, the new
optimum is applied with today's spread, unvalidated.

**Why some generalists rank lower.** Dandelion, sorrel, plantain, nettle and
rose hips in North Europe lose 0.02–0.04, and no spread recovers it: the
variants differ from each other by less than that. The optimum comes from
all-years sightings, but this year's records sit three to seven degrees further
north, mostly from the Nordic recording schemes, on more acid soil:

| species   | all years: median latitude, pH | 2026: median latitude, pH |
| --------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------- |
| Dandelion | 51.5° N, 6.1                   | 58.0° N, 5.4              |
| Sorrel    | 52.9° N, 5.9                   | 59.2° N, 5.1              |
| Plantain  | 52.3° N, 6.1                   | 59.0° N, 5.2              |
| Nettle    | 52.9° N, 6.1                   | 58.9° N, 5.3              |
| Rose hips | 53.0° N, 6.0                   | 56.0° N, 5.5              |

Sloes and walnut, whose two samples sit in the same place, gain clearly. For
the generalists the 2026 test mostly measures which countries published this
year. They keep the default and are worth a recheck on the next season's
records. Their saturation still falls: dandelion 32% → 17%, sorrel 32% → 22%.
Four mushrooms (hedgehog, matsutake, saffron milk cap and winter chanterelle in
North Europe) lose 0.01–0.02 on 58–438 sightings, within the noise. In South
Europe, blackberry, chicken of the woods and hazelnut lose 0.01, morel 0.03,
and sea buckthorn 0.03 even with its pH term softened to nearly flat (0.52 at
today's spread).

## Altitude

Altitude was tested the same way: the optimum at the median elevation of each
species' sightings, with today's spread, 400 m and 1,600 m. Against random
points, 29 of 43 North Europe species ranked better, nearly all with a lower,
sharper optimum. That rewards where people look rather than where species
grow. Against other sightings of the same kingdom inside the range, which
cancels that, the lower optimum made the altitude term worse for most species:
porcini 0.66 → 0.51, pine bolete 0.72 → 0.53, chanterelle 0.66 → 0.51, and
lingonberry in South Europe 0.79 → 0.56. Their current, higher optima carry a
real preference for higher ground than the average sighting, so they stay.
Altitude changes only where both tests agree:

| species               | region | optimal altitude | same-kingdom term | 2026 ranking |
| --------------------- | ------ | ---------------- | ----------------- | ------------ |
| Amaranth              | NE     | 500 → 20 m       | 0.43 → 0.56       | 0.51 → 0.53  |
| St. George's Mushroom | NE     | 500 → 40 m       | 0.50 → 0.53       | 0.57 → 0.60  |
| St. George's Mushroom | SE     | 500 → 180 m      | 0.47 → 0.50       | 0.68 → 0.71  |

The elevation the score uses is the fetched coordinate's, which is correct at
known places (Hardangervidda 1,231 m, Cairngorms 1,096 m). The base files' own
elevations are not: North Europe has zeros and blanks there, so the pipeline
keeps using the coordinate's.

## US

The US static file already reads SoilGrids (Spearman 0.95 with the raster at
the 181,106 US points), and most US pH terms work: inside each species' range,
sightings outrank other sightings of their kingdom on pH alone at 0.55–0.89.

## Rollout

The new static file is on R2 as `EU/EU_static_info_soilgrids.csv`; the old
`EU/EU_static_info.csv` is left in place. Merging switches `EU_STATIC_INFO`,
and the next North and South Europe scoring runs use the new pH with the new
parameters together. Reverting the `.env` line and the manifests restores the
old scores on the following run.
