# Motion

Three durations and one easing curve. Every transition in the application
references them; a hardcoded `200ms` is a bug even when the number is right,
because the next retune will not find it.

## Durations

Press **Run** to feel each one. Reading `150ms` off a table tells you nothing
about whether it is the right choice.

<Story of={MotionStories.Durations} />

Pick by what is moving, not by taste:

- **`--duration-fast` (150ms)** — a surface reacting under the pointer. Hover
  and press escalation, nothing that travels.
- **`--duration-base` (200ms)** — the default. If you are unsure, this is the
  answer.
- **`--duration-slow` (300ms)** — a large surface covering real distance, like
  a Sheet sliding in from an edge.

## Easing

<Story of={MotionStories.Easing} />

One curve for everything. `cubic-bezier(0.4, 0, 0.2, 1)` accelerates fast and
decelerates gently, so a surface reads as settling rather than snapping.

Adding a second easing token is a design decision, not a convenience — the
value of having one curve is that motion across the app feels like one system.

## Reduced motion

<Story of={MotionStories.ReducedMotion} />

When the viewer asks for reduced motion, animations and transitions collapse to
`0.01ms` rather than being removed. That distinction is deliberate: a
transition that never fires can leave a component wedged mid-state, while one
that completes instantly cannot. `elevation-interactive` goes further and drops
its transition altogether, since its whole purpose is the movement.

This is handled globally. Individual components do not need to check the
preference themselves.
